\chapter{Boosting shortest path multiplicity}
\label{ch:distance}

\section{The redundancy of shortest paths}

The small-world property, met back in the introduction, promises that any two
nodes of a real network are joined by a \emph{short} path --- only a few hops
apart. It says nothing, though, about how \emph{many} short paths there are.
That count turns out to matter a great deal. If the only shortest route between
two nodes runs through a single chain of intermediaries, the connection is
fragile: cut one link and the shortest route is gone. If instead there are
several shortest routes side by side, the connection is robust, and whatever
flows between the two nodes --- a message, a signal, a delay --- can travel along
all of them at once. The quantity that captures this is the \emph{shortest path
multiplicity}: the number of distinct shortest routes between a pair of nodes. A
simple picture: if two friends can each be reached from you in two handshakes,
but by two completely different chains of acquaintances, the multiplicity of that
connection is two. In real networks this multiplicity is conspicuously high.

And it is not an isolated fact. \citet{deng2026} found that, across many real
networks, the abundance of multiple shortest paths is strongly correlated with
\emph{community structure} --- the very groupings of the previous chapter --- more
strongly than with any other standard measure. Two networks of the same size and
the same degree distribution can differ widely in their path multiplicity, and
the difference tracks how community-rich they are. A correlation like that begs
for an explanation. Does community structure somehow \emph{cause} paths to
multiply, or do both spring from a common deeper source? The previous chapter
hands us the obvious suspect: local growth rules, which we saw conjure
communities out of nothing but locality. This closing chapter, following
\citet{vazquezpaths2026}, completes the case by showing that those same local
rules also \emph{boost} the multiplicity of shortest paths --- so that the
striking link between paths and communities is not one causing the other, but the
shared shadow cast by the rule that grew the network.

The mechanism is easy to picture once named. Local rules build short
\emph{cycles} --- closed loops --- as a matter of course: a triangle whenever a
friend introduces a friend, a square whenever a gene is copied. And a cycle is
exactly a structure that offers more than one way around. In a network with no
loops at all --- a \emph{tree} --- there is precisely one route between any two
nodes; the more loops a network carries, the more alternative shortest routes it
affords. Path multiplicity is simply what local rules leave behind when they
close loops.

\section{Defining the multiplicity}

We need only one quantity. Write $\mu_{ij}(G)$ for the number of distinct
shortest paths between nodes $i$ and $j$ in a network $G$ --- counting every route
that ties for the minimum number of hops. Averaging this over all pairs of nodes,
and over many networks grown the same way, gives the \emph{average shortest path
multiplicity} $\langle\mu\rangle$, the single number we will follow as the
network grows to size $n$. A tree, with its unique routes, has $\langle\mu\rangle
= 1$ exactly; anything above one measures the redundancy that loops provide. The
whole chapter is about one question: how fast does $\langle\mu\rangle$ rise as the
network gets bigger, and does that rate betray whether the network was grown by a
local rule?

\section{The baseline: random networks}

To judge what counts as ``boosted,'' we first need the expectation in the 
\emph{absence} of any local structure. The natural reference is a random 
network. For a dense random network --- the classic Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph, in 
which every possible link is present with some fixed probability --- the average 
multiplicity grows in proportion to the size $n$ \citep{dong2025}. But real 
networks are not dense; they are \emph{sparse}, each node having only a handful 
of links no matter how large the network. The fair baseline is therefore a 
\emph{sparse} random network, which we make by taking a network grown by a local 
rule and then shuffling its links at random while leaving each node's number of 
links untouched --- the same configuration-model scramble used as a control in 
the previous chapter. Stripped of their local order this way, these sparse 
random networks have a multiplicity that creeps up only \emph{logarithmically} 
with size, as determined by numerical simulations \citep{vazquezpaths2026}:

\begin{equation}
  \langle\mu\rangle_{S,0} = a + b\log n,
  \label{eq:path-baseline}
\end{equation}
the subscript $S,0$ marking a network that is sparse ($S$) and unstructured
($0$). Logarithmic growth is about as slow as growth gets --- to add a fixed
amount of multiplicity you must \emph{multiply} the network's size, not just add
to it. That sluggish, degrees-only baseline is the bar that local rules must
clear. Anything that grows faster is the doing of the rule.

Of note, the Barab\'asi--Albert model falls on this same baseline. Although it is
a \emph{growing} network rather than a randomized one, it has no local structure
--- newcomers attach across the whole graph, never consulting a neighborhood ---
and its shortest path multiplicity creeps up only logarithmically, exactly as the
reshuffled networks do. This echoes the previous chapter, where the very same
model failed to develop communities: lacking locality, it builds neither the
modules nor the redundant paths, and on both counts it behaves as though its links
had been shuffled at random.

\section{Local rules boost the multiplicity}

Now run the very same local models of the previous chapter \emph{without} 
shuffling --- the local-search rule, the duplication--split rule, the bubble 
model --- and the multiplicity climbs decisively faster than the logarithmic 
baseline. It helps to keep a ladder of growth rates in mind: logarithmic 
(slowest, the baseline), then \emph{log-quadratic} (faster), then 
\emph{exponential} (explosive, fastest of all). The local rules fall into two of 
these rungs, as deduced from numerical simulations \citep{vazquezpaths2026}.

Most of them --- local search and the bubble model among them --- show
\emph{log-quadratic} growth,
\begin{equation}
  \langle\mu\rangle_{S,I} = a + b\log n + c(\log n)^2,
  \label{eq:path-typeI}
\end{equation}
the extra squared term lifting the curve clearly above the straight logarithmic
baseline. Call this type I.

The duplication--split rule is different, and dramatically so: its multiplicity
grows \emph{exponentially} with size,
\begin{equation}
  \langle\mu\rangle_{S,II} = a\, e^{bn}, \qquad a,b>0,
  \label{eq:path-typeII}
\end{equation}
which we call type II. Exponential growth is a different world from logarithmic
--- the redundancy proliferates beyond anything the other rules manage. Copying,
it turns out, is the great multiplier of shortest paths, and that is what the
title of this chapter names.

\section{Cycles and the boost}

Why should duplication outrun the rest? The answer is in the kind of loop each
rule makes. The triadic-closure rule of the friends chapter closes
\emph{triangles}, loops of length three --- but in a triangle the two nodes
flanking a third are themselves directly linked, so there is still only \emph{one}
shortest route between any pair. A triangle adds cohesion but no extra routes.
Duplication, by contrast, closes \emph{squares}, loops of length four, and a
square is the smallest loop that genuinely offers a choice: its two opposite
corners are two hops apart by \emph{two} different paths, one around each side.
That single observation is the seed of the boost; the box pushes it a little
further, and the takeaway follows.

Figure~\ref{fig:path-square} makes the contrast concrete.

\begin{calculation}{why copying multiplies routes}
When a node is duplicated, the copy and the original share all their neighbors,
so any two of those neighbors sit at opposite corners of a four-cycle --- each
such pair instantly gains a second shortest route. A duplicated node of degree
$k$ creates $\tbinom{k}{2}=k(k-1)/2$ such squares in a single stroke. Moreover the
duplication rule carries its own preferential attachment, the chance of copying a
node being $\pi_k = qk/n$, which (as in Chapter~\ref{ch:duplication}) builds a
heavy-tailed degree distribution with exponent $\gamma = 1+1/q$. Squares are thus
laid down in proportion to $k(k-1)$ on top of a distribution with many
high-degree nodes --- a compounding that is the suspected origin of the
exponential law, Eq.~\eqref{eq:path-typeII}, though a full account of it remains
open.
\end{calculation}

The lesson of the box is that squares, not triangles, are what multiply shortest
routes: each duplication endows many pairs of nodes with a second equally-short
path, and because high-degree nodes (which spawn the most squares) are themselves
abundant, the redundancy compounds. The bubble model, which lets us dial the loop
length directly --- a chain of $L$ added nodes makes a loop of length $L+2$ ---
confirms the geometric intuition: even-length loops, whose opposite corners enjoy
two equal routes, enrich the network more than odd ones. (It is a useful caution,
though, that the bubble model's multiplicity still grows only log-quadratically,
type I, whatever the loop length; the square-counting explains why duplication
\emph{beats} triadic closure, but the full exponential ferocity of
duplication--split needs the compounding above.)

One more clue shows that randomness itself plays a role. The
Dorogovtsev--Goltsev--Mendes ``pseudofractal'' network
\citep{dorogovtsevgoltsev2002} is a \emph{deterministic} relative of the bubble
model --- grown by a fixed, clockwork rule rather than a random one. Its
multiplicity does \emph{not} accelerate into the log-quadratic regime; it grows
only log-linearly, like the shuffled baseline. So the speed-up in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:path-typeI} is not a property of the loop geometry alone --- it
requires the \emph{stochastic} character of the growth. It is the interplay of
randomness and locality together, not either by itself, that boosts the
multiplicity.

\section{Two sides of one coin}

Because local rules also create communities, and the number of communities grows
along with the network, we can ask how the multiplicity depends on the number of
communities directly. The relationship is a clean one --- linear for every local
model tested, and (once again the exception) exponential for duplication--split.
This is the empirical correlation of \citet{deng2026} restated from the inside:
more communities go hand in hand with more shortest paths.

But the point of the chapter is that this partnership is not a case of one
causing the other. ``Local rules boost shortest path multiplicity'' and
``communities accompany high shortest path multiplicity'' are two true statements
about the same networks, and as descriptions they say the same thing. Only one of
them, however, names a \emph{mechanism}. Communities are something an algorithm
reads off a finished network after the fact; the local rule is the process that
actually built that network, node by node, closing the very loops that both carve
it into communities and thread it with redundant routes. Community structure and
path multiplicity are two sides of a single coin --- and the coin is locality.

\section{Summary}

The shortest paths of real networks are richly redundant, and that redundancy
travels with their community structure. Sparse random networks, having no local
order, accumulate multiplicity only logarithmically as they grow; networks built
by local rules accumulate it far faster --- log-quadratically for most rules, and
exponentially for duplication, which closes the four-cycles that give opposite
corners two shortest routes apiece. Since the same local rules also produce the
communities, the observed tie between path multiplicity and communities is best
understood not as cause and effect but as two consequences of one underlying
local dynamics.
